# Facilitator guide: the leaders' session

The run of show for the facilitator. Read it once end to end, then keep
[the session on one page](#the-session-on-one-page) in front of you while you run it. What the
session is and who it is for is in the [README](README.md). The reasoning behind the design is in
[docs/DESIGN.md](../docs/DESIGN.md#same-topic-two-workshops).

**The spine, if you remember nothing else:** start with the decision this room has to make, teach
only enough to make it, get them voting early, show the failure, count what the room produced,
and finish with an owner and a date.

## The outcomes you are facilitating to

By the two-hour mark, the room can do these five things. If you have to cut something on the day,
cut what does not serve one of them.

| | Outcome | The evidence you look for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Judge** an AI answer by its evidence, not its confidence | The vote on three answers, and what people say they would check |
| 2 | **Ask** for the evidence behind a claim about an agent | The votes on the trace and the scoreboard. On the exit card: the question each leader will ask, of whom, by when |
| 3 | **Choose** a first pilot from their own workflows | A scorecard from every table, and one workflow on the wall |
| 4 | **Gate** it | A gate map with a person on every step that touches money, private information or a customer |
| 5 | **Commit** to it | The pilot plan: every row filled in, the owner's and the sponsor's names, a review date |

**Target for the room:** at the two-hour mark, every table has handed in a scorecard, the wall
holds one gate map and one plan with no blank rows, and the plan carries two names and a date.

## One week before

**The sponsor.** Find the person who owns the outcome, and ask three things. Can the people in
this room name a pilot and an owner without asking anyone else? What happens to the plan
afterward: is there someone to build it? And on what date, about 30 days later, will this group
look at the evidence? If the first answer is no, the room is wrong. Change the invitation list
before you change the session. The sponsor must be in the room on the day.

**The pre-session email.** Send the [pre-session email](handouts/pre-session-email.md), from the
sponsor if you can. It asks each leader for two workflows, a confidence score and their biggest
doubt. Chase replies two days before. Ask the sponsor for three spare workflows as well: someone
will arrive without one.

**The data rule.** The same [data rule](../docs/FACILITATOR.md#the-data-rule) as the engineers'
day. Nothing red goes on a slip, a card or the wall. Workflows are described, customers are not
named. Write the rule on the board, and say it at 0:00.

**Tables.** Three to five people at each, mixed by function. Do not seat a leader beside the
person they report to if you can avoid it. Put whoever owns risk, legal or compliance at a table,
not at the back.

**Staffing.** One facilitator can run 20 leaders, because nothing in the room can break. Above
12, add a scribe who keeps the wall, so you never turn your back on the room to write. Treat the
numbers as guidance, and adjust them for the room in front of you.

**Your own preparation.** The day before:

| Have ready | Why |
|---|---|
| Every [handout](handouts/README.md) printed, with the reveal sheets in a separate pile | The session runs on paper. A reveal seen early spoils a vote |
| The leaders' workflows, one per slip, dealt into a pile for each table | Block 4 starts in two minutes, not ten |
| Three spare workflows from the sponsor | Someone will arrive without one |
| The doubts from the replies, one per card, on the wall, without names | The skeptic's point is part of the session before the skeptic speaks |
| On the board: the data rule, the three votes (use, verify, reject), and the four suspects for Block 2 | You point. You do not write while you talk |
| The gate map and the pilot plan drawn large on the wall | The room's decision has to be visible to the room |
| A timer the room can see | Two hours has no slack |
| The date of the 30-day review, agreed with the sponsor | It goes on the plan before anyone leaves |
| You have run the engineers' labs, or you have read Labs 2, 3 and 5 and know who to ask | Leaders will ask how it works. The answers must be true |

## The session on one page

Minutes: 26 explaining and showing, 57 with the room working, 32 debriefing and deciding, and a
5-minute break.

## How every block runs

**Explain (two to five minutes).** One idea, in terms of a decision the leader already makes.
Never a definition. If you catch yourself past five minutes, stop and hand out the exhibit.

**Show.** Every block shows something, and every one has a failure in it. In Blocks 1 to 3 it is
a printed exhibit from the engineers' agent. In Blocks 4 and 5 it is the Ridgeline example on the
back of the handout. Each one says where in this repo it comes from, and which parts are an
illustration. If someone asks "is this real?", read that part out.

**The room works.** Four rules:

- Vote before you talk. Everyone decides alone, then all the cards go up together.
- Write every tally on the board. The tallies are your evidence, and the room's.
- Keep the reveal sheets back until the tally is written.
- Do not say which answer is right before the reveal. Ask one person on each side for one
  sentence, and say thank you.

**Debrief.** One question to the room, then the line to land, in those words or your own. Take
answers from tables, not from volunteers.

## Block by block

### 0:00 Open (5 minutes)

- **Say the outcome:** in two hours this room picks one pilot, names its owner and writes down
  what the pilot has to prove. One page leaves the room.
- **Read the phone call aloud.** "Can my 8-year-old take the belay class on Saturday? And can you
  book us in?" A fictional gym wants an AI to answer that. Engineers spend a day building it.
  This room looks at what it did.
- **Say the data rule,** and point at it.
- **Say how the room works:** you vote before you talk.
- **Point at the wall.** The doubts people sent are on it. Each one will become a test the pilot
  has to pass, or it stays on the wall for the review.

### 0:05 Block 1: Three answers (15 minutes)

Handout: [answer-cards.md](handouts/answer-cards.md).

- **Explain (3 minutes):** think of an agent as a capable new hire. It has a job description, a
  handbook and a set of keys. Here are three replies to the call. You are the gym's owner. Which
  would you let the caller hear?
- **Show:** the three cards. One minute of silent reading.
- **The room works (7 minutes):** take the cards one at a time. "Card A: votes up." Use, verify
  or reject. Write each tally on the board. For each card, ask one person on each side for one
  sentence.
- **The failure on purpose:** Card A is wrong twice, and it is the reply a customer would like
  best. If some of the room votes to use it, that is the lesson arriving. If nobody does, ask who
  would have been pleased to receive it as the parent.
- **Debrief (5 minutes):** hand out the reveal sheet. Ask: "Without the handbook, what on the
  card could have told you?" Then: "Your system does not know the answer. Which of these three do
  you want it to say?"
- **Watch for:** tables that talk before they vote. And a room that calls Card B useless: agree
  that the caller got nothing, and say the next block is about why.
- **Line to land:** fluent is not the same as right. Trust an answer for what you can check: a
  source it names, an action that waits for a yes, an honest "I'm not sure".

### 0:20 Block 2: Whose fault was it? (13 minutes)

Handout: [trace-exhibit.md](handouts/trace-exhibit.md).

- **Explain (1 minute), then the first vote:** Card B again. The caller asked a clear question
  and got no answer and no booking. The tester scored it as bad. Whose fault was it? Point at the
  four suspects on the board: the model, the instructions, the knowledge, the caller. Hands up for
  each. Write the tally.
- **Show (3 minutes):** hand out the trace. A trace is the record of one turn: what the agent was
  told, what it was asked, what it said, how long it took, what it cost. Engineers get one for
  every answer.
- **The room works (5 minutes):** two minutes alone with a pen. Underline the sentence that says
  who may take the belay class, and the sentence the agent was following when it said "I'm not
  sure". Then vote again, and write the second tally under the first.
- **The failure on purpose:** the first sentence does not exist. The system message holds
  instructions and the caller's name, and nothing about classes.
- **Debrief (4 minutes):** hand out the reveal sheet. The answer is the knowledge. Then the
  analogy: an open-book exam. Ridgeline sent a new hire to the phone without the handbook, and
  the new hire did the right thing. The fix was to give it the book: before each answer, the
  agent now looks up the three closest handbook sections and reads them first. Ask: "When a team
  tells you the model hallucinated, what will you ask to see?"
- **Watch for:** someone who still blames the model after reading. Ask them to point at the
  sentence the model should have used.
- **Line to land:** the model did not fail. It never had the fact. "The agent is wrong" becomes
  "which part was wrong?"

### 0:33 Block 3: Would you have shipped it? (14 minutes)

Handout: [scoreboard-exhibit.md](handouts/scoreboard-exhibit.md).

- **Explain (2 minutes):** callers said the answers were too long. Someone rewrote the
  instructions: be brief. They tried it in a chat window, and it felt better.
- **Show (2 minutes):** hand out Part 1, with both sets of instructions.
- **The room works (5 minutes):** one minute alone. Then the vote: ship, do not ship, or not
  yet. Anyone who says "not yet" has to say what they want to see first. Write the tally. Hand
  out Part 2 and give one minute to read the first table.
- **The failure on purpose:** version 2 scored worse on honesty (2 of 2 down to 0 of 2) and on
  safety (1 of 1 down to 0 of 1). Nobody saw it in the chat window.
- **Debrief (5 minutes):** the analogy is the driving test before you hand over the car: the same
  19 test calls, marked the same way, after every change. Ask: "What did 'be brief' take out?"
  Then read the second table down: no rewording moved knowledge or booking. The handbook and the
  tools did. Ask: "Before the next change to an agent ships here, what will you ask to see?"
- **Watch for:** someone who caught it by reading the two prompts side by side. Say well done,
  then ask how they would catch one changed line in fifty pages. And someone who says 19 calls is
  not many: agree. The handout says so, and says what a real pilot needs.
- **Line to land:** you cannot judge an agent one chat at a time. A change ships on its scores,
  not on its demo.

### 0:47 Break (5 minutes)

- Put a scorecard at every seat, and the pile of workflow slips and the example on every table.
- Breaks with senior people run long. Start Block 4 at 0:52 with whoever is in the room.

### 0:52 Block 4: Score your own workflows (33 minutes)

Handout: [workflow-scorecard.md](handouts/workflow-scorecard.md).

- **Explain (4 minutes):** Ridgeline goes back in the folder. From here, everything on the table
  belongs to the room. Four scores, 1 to 5, and 5 is always good news for a first pilot: business value,
  whether the data is ready, whether a mistake is cheap, whether it is easy to undo. Then the
  floors: a workflow that scores 1 or 2 on mistakes, on undoing or on data is not a first pilot,
  whatever its total.
- **Show:** the Ridgeline example on the back. Three ideas, and the one that sounds best in a
  meeting scores lowest.
- **The room works (20 minutes):** two minutes to pick two or three slips and strike out anything
  a form, a rule or a search box would solve. Eight minutes to score alone, in silence. Ten
  minutes to compare, agree a score for each row, apply the floors and circle a winner.
- **The failure on purpose:** the most exciting workflow on each table. Ask every table which
  idea it was, and which row sank it.
- **Debrief and decide (9 minutes):** one minute per table: the winner, its four scores, and the
  idea it parked. The scribe writes the winners on the wall, highest total first. Then the room
  chooses one, by a show of hands. The sponsor confirms it, or overrules it and says why. Then
  ask: "Who owns this?" Wait for a name. Write it on the wall. The other winners stay on the wall
  as the shortlist, each with a name beside it.
- **Watch for:** a table scored by its loudest member (ask to see the individual cards). A
  workflow that is really three (ask for the smallest piece that would still be worth having). A
  5 for data on something nobody has written down. A workflow that needs red data: it scores 1.
- **Line to land:** the best first pilot is rarely the most exciting one. It is valuable, its
  data is ready, and it is safe to try.

### 1:25 Block 5: Place the approval gates (18 minutes)

Handout: [gate-map.md](handouts/gate-map.md).

- **Explain and show (4 minutes):** turn the handout over to the Ridgeline example. The model
  proposes, and code and people decide. Read out the booking that stopped: the model asked to
  book, the code booked nothing and sent back a summary, the caller said yes, and only then did
  the booking go through. An agent is a new hire with a limited set of keys. Ridgeline's has six,
  and none of them opens refunds.
- **The failure on purpose:** Card A again. If the finished agent tried to make that booking,
  the code would answer: "Intro to Belay is for ages 12+; Sam is 8." Then point at step 7,
  injuries. That rule lives in a sentence, and version 2 deleted the sentence.
- **The room works (10 minutes):** for three minutes, the owner lists the steps of the chosen
  workflow on the wall, five to eight of them, and the room corrects. For seven minutes, each
  table copies the steps, ticks M, P and C, and writes A, H or X on every step. Every H and X
  gets a role, and a note of where the rule lives.
- **Debrief (4 minutes):** go down the wall map. Stop only where tables differ. The owner
  decides each difference, and the scribe writes the result. Ask: "Which gate will people ask to
  remove first, and what evidence would you need before you agreed?"
- **Watch for:** a gate written as "the agent will be told to ask". That is a sentence, not a
  gate. A map with no person-only step: look harder. A table that gates nothing because "it is
  only a pilot".
- **Line to land:** a rule that matters lives in code or in a person's approval. A sentence in
  the instructions lasts until the next edit.

### 1:43 Block 6: The pilot plan (12 minutes)

Handout: [pilot-plan.md](handouts/pilot-plan.md).

- **Explain (2 minutes):** one page, seven rows. A row with no name, no number or no date is not
  finished. Share out the rows: the owner's table takes rows 1 to 3, one table takes the test
  cases, one takes rows 5 and 6, one takes row 7 and the shortlist. A fifth table joins the test
  cases. With two tables, split the page in half.
- **The room works (10 minutes):** tables write. Whoever has the test cases and row 6 takes the
  doubts off the wall: each doubt becomes a test case or a reason to stop. As rows are finished,
  the scribe copies them onto the wall plan.
- **The failure on purpose:** a blank. "Baseline: to be confirmed" is a blank. Read it aloud and
  ask who will count it, and by when.
- **Watch for:** a team named as the owner. A target with no number. A stop condition nobody
  could ever observe.
- **Line to land:** a pilot with no owner is a demo.

### 1:55 Close (5 minutes)

- The owner reads the plan aloud, from the wall. One minute.
- The owner and the sponsor sign it. The sponsor says the date of the review, and what happens
  there: the owner answers the five questions with evidence, and the group decides whether to go
  on, change it or stop.
- Hand out the [five questions](../handouts/questions-for-leaders.md) and the
  [exit card](handouts/exit-card.md). Two minutes to fill in the card.
- Photograph the wall. Collect the scorecards, the gate maps and the exit cards.
- Say what happens next: the plan by email tomorrow, the baseline counted in week one, the review
  on the date on the wall.

## Plain words for the questions leaders ask

Teach each idea through the decision it changes, not through a definition. These are accurate for
the agent in this repo.

| They ask about | Say it like this | The decision it changes |
|---|---|---|
| What an agent is | A capable new hire: a job description (the instructions), a handbook (the knowledge), a limited set of keys (the actions it may take), and a test before it works alone | Which job, which handbook, which keys |
| Retrieval, or RAG | An open-book exam. Before it answers, the agent looks up the closest pages and reads them. It searches by meaning: in the engineers' lab, a question about a "nanny" finds the waiver rule, which says "babysitter". It can only be as right as the book | Who owns the book, and is it up to date? |
| Hallucination | A confident answer with nothing behind it. Often the agent was never given the fact, or was never told to say "I'm not sure" | Ask for the trace before you blame the model |
| A trace | The record of one answer: what the agent was told, what it looked up, what it did, what it said, the time and the cost | Can the team show one for an answer that went wrong? |
| Evals | The driving test before you hand over the car. A fixed set of cases, marked the same way every time, taken again after every change | No change ships without the scores before and after |
| The judge | A second model that marks the answers. It is an examiner, so it is examined first. Ridgeline's agreed with a person on 12 of 12 answers before its scores were used | Who checked the grader? |
| Tools | The keys. Each one opens one door: check the schedule, book a class. No key, no door | Which keys does the pilot get on the first day? |
| Guardrails | Locks, not instructions. Checks in code that run whatever the model says | What can it do without a person, and where is that enforced? |
| MCP | A universal adapter: one standard plug, so that other AI assistants can use the same tools. The locks are on the tools, not in the assistant, so they still hold | Before another team's assistant, or a supplier's, plugs into your systems: what can it do without a person? |
| Which model to buy | Not the first question. Every row of Ridgeline's scoreboard used the same model. What moved the scores was the rules, the handbook and the tools | Ask what kind of problem it is before paying for a bigger model |
| Agent, or something simpler | If the steps are always the same, write them down and let the model fill them in. Use an agent only where the next step depends on what just happened. Start with the simplest thing that works | Does this pilot need an agent at all? |

## When things go wrong

| What happens | What to do |
|---|---|
| **A leader says "this is all hype"** | Thank them, and agree with the part that is true: a demo proves nothing, and the first three blocks are three ways a demo misleads. Then turn the doubt into a test: "What would it have to get right, or never get wrong, before you trusted it?" Write the answer on a card and put it on the wall. In Block 6 it becomes a test case. Skeptics are often the best people in the room once their doubt is part of the work. |
| **The sponsor wants slides only** | Ask what they want these leaders to do differently afterward. If the answer is "choose a pilot", a talk cannot produce that. Offer ten minutes as proof: run Three answers at the start of their own meeting and let the vote make the case. If the session has to shrink, shorten Blocks 1 to 3. Blocks 4 to 6 are the outcome. |
| **One voice dominates** | The format is on your side: votes go up together, and scores are written alone before anyone speaks. At each table, ask the most senior person to report last. If one person is still scoring for a whole table, ask to see the individual cards side by side. |
| **Nobody brought a workflow** | Start with the sponsor's three spares. Then give the room three silent minutes and one question: "What did your team do last week that it also did the week before?" One slip each. If a table is still empty, offer the usual candidates: answering the same policy questions from staff or customers, sorting incoming requests, writing routine replies for a person to send, checking a document against a checklist, preparing a weekly report. |
| **The room cannot agree on one pilot** | That is the sponsor's decision, and the reason the sponsor is in the room. The runner-up goes to the top of the shortlist, with a name. |
| **The winning workflow needs red data** | It was scored too high. Red data is a 1 for "the data is ready", and a 1 is under the floor. The first project there is the approval. Take the next workflow. |
| **Nobody will own it** | Do not write a team's name in the box. Say it plainly: a pilot with no owner is a demo. Ask the sponsor who decides, and write that name, with a date for the decision, as the first line of the plan. |
| **Someone wants to automate the whole process** | Ask for the smallest piece that would still be worth having, and score that. |
| **Someone asks which model or supplier to buy** | Park it on the wall. The plan does not depend on the answer. The five questions work on a supplier as well as on a team. |
| **Someone asks a technical question you cannot answer** | Say that you are not sure, write it on the wall, and answer by email. That is Card B, and the room has just voted on what it thinks of Card B. |
| **You are ten minutes late at the break** | Cut the table reports in Block 4 to 30 seconds each. In Block 6, the owner's table writes the whole plan while the other tables turn doubts into test cases. Do not cut the reading aloud or the signatures. |
| **The room is from several organisations** | Each table chooses its own pilot and writes its own plan. The wall holds one plan per table. Everything else stays the same. |

## Measuring the session

| When | What you collect | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Before | Two workflows per leader, the confidence score, the doubts, the sponsor's three answers | Whether the room can decide; a baseline for confidence; what the room is worried about |
| During | The tally of every vote, and the checkpoint in every block | Whether each exhibit did its work, while you can still do something about it |
| At the two-hour mark | Scorecards, the gate map, the signed plan, [exit cards](handouts/exit-card.md) | Whether the five outcomes happened |
| Week one | Whether the baseline was counted and the plan was handed to a builder | Whether the plan survived contact with the calendar |
| Day 30 | The review, and one email to every leader: did you ask your question, and what came back? | The number that matters |
| Day 60 to 90 | The sponsor check | Whether a second pilot starts, or the builders need a day of their own |

**The tally sheet.** Fill it in as you go. It is the record of the session.

| Block | Count |
|---|---|
| 1 | Use, verify and reject, for each of the three cards |
| 2 | Votes for each suspect, before the trace and after it |
| 3 | Ship, do not ship, not yet. How many asked for a test before they saw the scores |
| 4 | Workflows scored. Workflows that fell under a floor. Tables that handed in a scorecard |
| 5 | Steps on the map. Steps with a tick. Steps that are person only |
| 6 | Rows of the plan filled in. Doubts turned into test cases. Doubts left on the wall |

**The number to report:** whether the room left with one pilot that has an owner, a baseline to
count and a review date, and whether, at day 30, the pilot had started and the owner could answer
the five questions with evidence. A review that ends in "stop" counts as a success if the
evidence says stop.

**The funnel for the sponsor:** invited → attended → sent a workflow → scored → pilot chosen with
an owner → started within 30 days → evidence shown at the review → decision made.

**After each room,** read the "least clear" answers on the exit cards. Anything that appears three
times becomes a change to a handout or to this guide before the next room. If a vote went the
same way before and after its reveal, the exhibit did not do its work: look at how it was
introduced.

The emails for every step are in [handouts/follow-up-emails.md](handouts/follow-up-emails.md).

## Variant: with the agent running

The session needs no laptop. If you have run the engineers' labs and have the solution working,
you may show two things live, inside the explain-and-show minutes of the block and never beyond
them.

- **Block 2:** from `solution/`, run `uv run python -m gym_agent.chat --no-tools --no-memory`,
  ask the phone-call question, and open the trace in Langfuse. Then run it without the two flags
  and ask again.
- **Block 3:** open the experiment comparison in Langfuse, with the three prompt versions side by
  side.

Keep the paper. A live reply will be worded differently from the card, the room still needs
something to write on, and the network cannot break paper.
